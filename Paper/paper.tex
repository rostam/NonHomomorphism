\begin{abstract}\sloppy
The \emph{non-homomorphism factor} $|G,H|$ is the minimum number of edges that
must be removed from $G$ so that the remaining graph admits a homomorphism to
$H$.  The \emph{concentration parameter} $\gamma(G,H)$ is the minimum, over all
homomorphisms $f\colon G\to H$, of the largest number of edges of $G$ that $f$
sends onto a single edge of $H$.

For $|G,H|$ we prove that, for the Hamming graph $H(d,n)=K_n^{\,\square d}$,
\[|H(d,n),H|=d\,n^{d-1}\Bigl(\binom n2-t\bigl(n,\omega(H)\bigr)\Bigr).\]
So on complete graphs, rook's graphs and all Hamming graphs only the clique
number of the target matters.  For every edge-transitive
$G$ and every $F$ with $F\to G$ and $F\not\to H$ we prove
$|G,H|\ge|E(G)|/|E(F)|$.  An averaging argument gives the general upper bound
$|V(H)|^2\,|G,H|\le|E(G)|\,(|V(H)|^2-2|E(H)|)$.  For a $d$-regular graph $G$ on $n$
vertices and $K_{r+1}$-free $H$ we prove $|L(G),H|\ge n\bigl(\binom d2-t(d,r)\bigr)$,
with equality for every $H$ of clique number $r$ when $G$ has a proper
$d$-edge-colouring.  This gives exact ground-state energies on the kagome,
checkerboard and pyrochlore lattices for every target.  We determine the max-$r$-cut of the triangular graphs $J(n,2)$ for all $n$ and
$r$; a parity obstruction costs exactly $r/2$ edges when $n$ is odd and $n-1$ is an
odd multiple of $r$, and $J(n,2)$ with $n\equiv3\pmod4$ has semidefinite
integrality gap exactly one.  For odd $n$ the folded
cube satisfies $|FQ_n,H|=2^{n-2}$ for every $H$ with an edge into which $C_n$ does
not map.
For $r=2$ equality holds when $G$ has a balanced $2$-edge-colouring.  We prove the quantitative Hedetniemi bound
\[|G\times H,K|\le\min(2|E(H)|\,|G,K|,\,2|E(G)|\,|H,K|)\]
and the exact value $|C_p\times C_q,H|=2p$ for odd $p\le q$ and every target
$H$ with an edge into which $C_q$ does not map, for instance every odd cycle $C_m$
with $m>q$.  For cubic $G$ we show that
$|L(G),L(P)|$, where $P$ is the Petersen graph, is at least the number of leaf
blocks of the bridge-block tree of $G$.

For $\gamma$ we prove multiplicativity, monotonicity, and the transfer bound
$|G,K|\le\gamma(G,H)\,|H,K|$.  We find an exact identity for the loads of a
homomorphism to $K_3$ in terms of the degree sums of its colour classes.  From it,
every $d$-regular graph on $n$ vertices with an equitable $3$-colouring has
$\gamma(G,K_3)=\frac d2\bigl(\lceil n/3\rceil+[n\equiv2\ (3)]\bigr)$.  With the
Chen--Lih--Wu theorem this gives $\gamma(G,K_3)=3\lceil|V(G)|/6\rceil$ for every
connected cubic graph other than $K_4$ and $K_{3,3}$: prisms, M\"obius ladders,
generalized Petersen graphs and the Petersen graph itself.  For hypercubes,
$\gamma(Q_d,K_3)=d(2^d+2)/6$ for even $d$ and $d(2^d+4)/6$ for odd $d$.  For odd cycles as targets we show that every $d$-regular
$G$ satisfies $\gamma(G,C_m)\ge\frac d2M_m(n)$, where $M_m(n)$ is the least
$M\equiv n\pmod 2$ with $mM\ge n$; this is exact for cycles and tori.  We also
determine $\gamma(\cdot,K_3)$ for complete bipartite graphs, books, friendship
graphs, wheels and complete tripartite graphs.  For odd $m$ we determine
$\gamma(C_n,C_m)$ exactly: it is the least $M\equiv n\pmod 2$ with $mM\ge n$.  We also show that $\max(|G,H|,|H,G|)$ is not a pseudo-metric.

Every result in this paper is formally verified in the Lean~4 proof assistant
with the Mathlib library.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

All graphs are finite, simple and undirected.

\begin{definition}[Graph homomorphism]
A \emph{homomorphism} from $G$ to $H$ is a map $f\colon V(G)\to V(H)$ such that
$\{u,v\}\in E(G)$ implies $\{f(u),f(v)\}\in E(H)$.  We write $G\to H$ when such
a map exists, and $G\leftrightarrow H$ when $G\to H$ and $H\to G$.
\end{definition}

A proper $k$-colouring of $G$ is the same as a homomorphism $G\to K_k$.  Two
standard facts~\cite{hell2004} follow directly.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:chi_mono}
If $G\to H$ then $\chi(G)\le\chi(H)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:hom_contracts}
Every homomorphism $f\colon G\to H$ satisfies $d_H(f(u),f(v))\le d_G(u,v)$ for
all $u,v\in V(G)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A walk of length $\ell$ from $u$ to $v$ maps to a walk of length $\ell$ from
$f(u)$ to $f(v)$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Formal verification.}
Every theorem, lemma, proposition and corollary in this paper has been proved in
Lean~4~\cite{lean4} using Mathlib~\cite{mathlib}.  The only exceptions are the
classical results quoted in Remark~\ref{rem:balanced}, the Chen--Lih--Wu
theorem used in Corollary~\ref{cor:cubic}, K\H{o}nig's theorem in
Corollary~\ref{cor:lattices}, and the computer searches, which are marked as such.  The development is in
the \texttt{lean/NonHom} directory of the repository.
Section~\ref{sec:formal} describes it and lists the Lean name of each result.

\paragraph{Related work.}
The quantity $|E(G)|-|G,H|$ is the optimum of the \emph{maximum $H$-colourable
subgraph} problem (Max $H$-Col), a restriction of Max CSP studied by Jonsson,
F\"arnqvist, Thapper and others~\cite{fjt2009,efjt2009}.  For $H=K_k$ it is the
max-$k$-cut.  F\"arnqvist, Jonsson and Thapper introduced the parameter
$s(H,G)$, which for edge-transitive $G$ equals the largest $H$-colourable
fraction of $E(G)$; our Theorem~\ref{thm:edge_transitive} is the corresponding
averaging bound and should be read as a reformulation of their argument.
Eigenvalue and semidefinite bounds for the max-$k$-cut go back to Mohar and
Poljak~\cite{mohar1990}; van Dam and Sotirov~\cite{vandam2016} proved them tight
for some graphs of the Hamming scheme with $k=q$.  We found no exact max-$k$-cut
values in the literature for the Cartesian Hamming graphs with $k<q$, for the
triangular graphs $J(n,2)$, or for tensor products of odd cycles, and no
treatment of the concentration parameter $\gamma$.

\section{Non-Homomorphism Factors}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{definition}[Non-homomorphism factor~\cite{khoshkha2005}]
\label{def:nhf}
$|G,H|$ is the minimum number of edges that must be removed from $G$ to obtain a
graph $G'$ with $G'\to H$.  In particular $|G,H|=0$ if and only if $G\to H$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Complexity]
For fixed $H$, deciding $|G,H|\le k$ is \textsc{Edge Deletion-$H$-Colouring},
studied by Foucaud, Hocquard, Lajou, Mitsou and Pierron~\cite{foucaud2019}.  The
case $k=0$ is \textsc{$H$-Colouring}.  So by the Hell--Ne\v{s}et\v{r}il
dichotomy~\cite{hell2004}, computing $|G,H|$ is NP-hard whenever the core of $H$
has more than one edge.  For $H=K_2$ the problem is \textsc{Edge Bipartization},
which is NP-complete but fixed-parameter tractable in $k$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[\cite{khoshkha2005}]
\label{prop:monotone}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item\label{it:mon1} If $H_1\to H_2$ then $|G,H_1|\ge|G,H_2|$ for every $G$.
  \item\label{it:mon2} If $H_1\leftrightarrow H_2$ then $|G,H_1|=|G,H_2|$ for every $G$.
  \item\label{it:mon3} If $f\colon G_1\to G_2$ is edge-injective (distinct edges
        map to distinct edges) then $|G_1,H|\le|G_2,H|$ for every $H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(\ref{it:mon1}) If $G'\to H_1$ then $G'\to H_1\to H_2$.
(\ref{it:mon2}) Apply (\ref{it:mon1}) in both directions.
(\ref{it:mon3}) Take $S\subseteq E(G_2)$ with $|S|=|G_2,H|$ and $G_2-S\to H$, and
let $S'=\{e\in E(G_1):f(e)\in S\}$.  Edge-injectivity gives $|S'|\le|S|$, and $f$
restricts to a homomorphism $G_1-S'\to G_2-S$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Complete graphs and Tur\'an's theorem}

Let $t(n,m)$ be the number of edges of the Tur\'an graph $T(n,m)$, the complete
$m$-partite graph on $n$ vertices with parts as equal as possible.  By Tur\'an's
theorem, every $K_{m+1}$-free graph on $n$ vertices has at most $t(n,m)$ edges.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:KnKm}
For $m\ge1$, $\displaystyle|K_n,K_m| = \binom{n}{2} - t(n,m)$.
If $n=qm+r$ with $0\le r<m$, this equals
$r\dbinom{q+1}{2}+(m-r)\dbinom{q}{2}$.
In particular $|K_{n+1},K_n|=1$ for all $n\ge1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Removing the edges outside $T(n,m)$ leaves an $m$-colourable graph.  Conversely,
an $m$-colourable spanning subgraph of $K_n$ is $K_{m+1}$-free, so by Tur\'an's
theorem it has at most $t(n,m)$ edges.  The explicit formula follows by induction
on $q$ from $t(n+m,m)=t(n,m)+n(m-1)+\binom m2$.  For $n+1=1\cdot n+1$ it gives
$\binom22=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:lower_bound}
For all graphs $G$ and $H$,
$|G,H|\ge \max\bigl(0,\,|E(G)|-t(|V(G)|,\chi(H))\bigr)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $G'\subseteq G$ and $G'\to H$ then $\chi(G')\le\chi(H)$, so $G'$ is
$K_{\chi(H)+1}$-free and has at most $t(|V(G)|,\chi(H))$ edges.
\end{proof}

For complete sources, the target matters only through its clique number.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:Kn_clique}
Let $H$ have clique number $r\ge1$.  Then $|K_n,H|=\binom n2-t(n,r)$.  In
particular $|K_n,H|=\binom n2-\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ for every triangle-free $H$
with an edge.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A homomorphism maps a clique injectively onto a clique.  So if $G'\to H$ then
$G'$ is $K_{r+1}$-free, and by Tur\'an's theorem $|E(G')|\le t(n,r)$.
Conversely $T(n,r)\to K_r\to H$.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:KnKm} is the case $H=K_m$.  For example $|K_n,C_{2k+1}|$ and
$|K_n,P|$ do not depend on $k$ or on the Petersen structure.  They all equal
$|K_n,K_2|$, while $|K_n,K_3|$ is strictly smaller for $n\ge3$.

The same holds for every Hamming graph.  The \emph{Hamming graph} $H(d,n)$ has
vertex set $\{0,\dots,n-1\}^d$, two words being adjacent if they differ in exactly
one coordinate.  So $H(1,n)=K_n$, $H(2,n)=K_n\square K_n$ is the $n\times n$
rook's graph, and $H(d,2)=Q_d$ is the hypercube.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:hamming}
Let $H$ have clique number $r\ge1$.  Then
\[
  |H(d,n),H| = d\,n^{d-1}\left(\binom n2-t(n,r)\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A \emph{line} is the set of $n$ words that agree outside one fixed coordinate.
Every line induces $K_n$, and every edge lies on exactly one line, so $E(H(d,n))$
is partitioned into the edge sets of $d\,n^{d-1}$ copies of $K_n$.  If
$H(d,n)-S\to H$, the kept part of each line is $K_{r+1}$-free.  By Tur\'an's
theorem each line therefore loses at least $\binom n2-t(n,r)$ edges.

For the upper bound, colour a word $x$ by $\bigl(\sum_i x_i\bmod n\bigr)\bmod r$.
This is a homomorphism to $K_r$, hence to $H$, after deleting the monochromatic
edges.  Along a line the value $\sum_i x_i\bmod n$ runs through all residues
exactly once.  So the colouring of each line is a relabelled Tur\'an graph
$T(n,r)$, and exactly $\binom n2-t(n,r)$ of its edges are monochromatic.
\end{proof}

For example, the $n\times n$ rook's graph has $|K_n\square K_n,K_2|=2n\bigl(\binom
n2-\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor\bigr)$.  Every triangle-free target with an edge, such as
$C_5$ or $P$, gives the same value, while $|K_n\square K_n,K_3|=2n\bigl(\binom n2-t(n,3)\bigr)$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:Kn_clique} is the case $d=1$.

The density bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower_bound} has a matching upper bound.
It comes from a uniformly random map.

\begin{theorem}[Averaging bound]
\label{thm:average}
For all graphs $G$ and every graph $H$ with at least one vertex,
\[
  |V(H)|^2\,|G,H|\;\le\;|E(G)|\,\bigl(|V(H)|^2-2|E(H)|\bigr).
\]
In particular $|G,K_m|\le|E(G)|/m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a map $f\colon V(G)\to V(H)$, delete the edges whose image is not an edge.  An
edge $uv$ of $G$ survives for exactly $2|E(H)|\cdot|V(H)|^{|V(G)|-2}$ maps $f$.
So the average number of deleted edges is $|E(G)|(1-2|E(H)|/|V(H)|^2)$, and some
map does at least this well.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cycles and the Petersen graph}

Every cycle is $3$-colourable, so $|C_n,K_3|=0$.  For $K_2$, $|C_n,K_2|=0$ if
$n$ is even and $|C_n,K_2|=1$ if $n\ge3$ is odd.  Indeed an odd cycle is not
bipartite, and deleting one edge leaves a path.

Let $P$ be the Petersen graph, with outer cycle $u_0u_1u_2u_3u_4$, spokes
$u_iv_i$, and inner edges $v_iv_{i+2}$ (indices mod $5$).  It has $10$ vertices,
$15$ edges, and $\chi(P)=3$, so $|P,K_m|=0$ for $m\ge3$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:PK2}
$|P,K_2|=3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$P$ has exactly twelve $5$-cycles, and each edge lies on exactly four of them.
Two edges therefore lie on at most eight, so after deleting any two edges some
$5$-cycle survives and the graph is not bipartite.  Hence $|P,K_2|\ge3$.
Conversely, deleting $u_0u_1$, $u_3v_3$ and $v_2v_4$ leaves a bipartite graph
with sides $\{u_0,u_1,u_3,v_2,v_3,v_4\}$ and $\{u_2,u_4,v_0,v_1\}$.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:lower_bound} only gives $|P,K_2|\ge15-t(10,2)<0$ here, so the
odd-cycle structure, not edge density, is what forces the deletions.

\subsection{Edge-transitive graphs}

A graph $G$ is \emph{edge-transitive} if its automorphism group acts
transitively on $E(G)$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:edge_transitive}
Let $G$ be edge-transitive.  Let $F$ be a graph with at least one edge such that
$F\to G$ and $F\not\to H$.  Then
\[
  |G,H|\;\ge\;\frac{|E(G)|}{|E(F)|}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\varphi\colon F\to G$ and let $\Gamma=\operatorname{Aut}(G)$.  For an edge $e$
of $G$, put $c(e)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Gamma}\mu(\sigma\circ\varphi,e)$.  Reindexing
by $\sigma\mapsto\tau^{-1}\sigma$ gives $c(\tau e)=c(e)$.  So by
edge-transitivity $c$ is a constant $c_0$, and summing over all edges gives
$|E(G)|\,c_0=|\Gamma|\,|E(F)|$.

Now let $G-S\to H$.  Since $F\not\to H$, each $\sigma\circ\varphi$ maps some edge
of $F$ into $S$, so $|\Gamma|\le\sum_{e\in S}c(e)=|S|\,c_0$.  Hence
$|E(G)|\le|E(F)|\,|S|$.
\end{proof}

With $F$ an odd cycle and $H=K_2$, this recovers $|P,K_2|\ge15/5=3$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:PK2}).  It also recovers $|C_p\times C_q,K_2|\ge2pq/q=2p$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:CpCq}), since $C_p\times C_q$ is edge-transitive and contains
closed odd walks of length $q$.  Similarly, for an edge-transitive $G$ containing
$K_{r+1}$ and a $K_{r+1}$-free $H$, the theorem gives
$|G,H|\ge|E(G)|/\binom{r+1}2$.  Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:average}, the
parameter $|G,H|$ of an edge-transitive graph is pinned between two linear
functions of $|E(G)|$.

The bound is sharp on the folded cubes.  The \emph{folded cube} $FQ_n$ has vertex
set $\{0,1\}^{n-1}$, two words being adjacent if they differ in exactly one
coordinate or in all coordinates.  It is the quotient of $Q_n$ by antipodal
pairs, has $2^{n-1}$ vertices and $n2^{n-2}$ edges, and for odd $n$ its shortest
odd cycle has length $n$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:folded}
Let $n\ge3$ be odd, and let $H$ have an edge with $C_n\not\to H$.  Then
\[
  |FQ_n,H| = 2^{n-2}.
\]
In particular $|FQ_n,K_2|=2^{n-2}$ and $|FQ_n,C_m|=2^{n-2}$ for every odd $m>n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For each vertex $x$, flip the coordinates $1,\dots,n-1$ one at a time and then
complement.  This is a closed walk of length $n$, so a homomorphism
$C_n\to FQ_n$, and it must use a deleted edge.  An edge determines the step at
which a walk uses it, by the set of coordinates its ends differ in, and then $x$
up to the choice of endpoint.  So each edge lies on at most two of the $2^{n-1}$
walks, and $|S|\ge2^{n-2}$.  Conversely, deleting the $2^{n-2}$ antipodal edges
leaves $Q_{n-1}$, which is bipartite, and $K_2\to H$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Line graphs of regular graphs}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:line}
Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices and let $H$ be $K_{r+1}$-free.  Then
\[
  |L(G),H|\;\ge\; n\left(\binom d2-t(d,r)\right).
\]
For $r=2$ this reads $|L(G),H|\ge n\lfloor(d-1)^2/4\rfloor$.  Suppose moreover
that $H$ has an edge and that $E(G)$ has a $2$-colouring that is
\emph{balanced}, meaning that at every vertex the two colours occur
$\lfloor d/2\rfloor$ and $\lceil d/2\rceil$ times.  Then equality holds for
every triangle-free $H$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The $d$ edges at a vertex $v$ form a clique $Q_v\cong K_d$ in $L(G)$.  Distinct
cliques share no edge of $L(G)$, since two distinct edges of $G$ share at most one
vertex.  If $L(G)-S\to H$ then $L(G)-S$ is $K_{r+1}$-free.  By Tur\'an's theorem
it keeps at most $t(d,r)$ of the $\binom d2$ edges of each $Q_v$.  Summing over
$v$ gives the lower bound.

For the upper bound, colour the vertices of $L(G)$ by the given edge colouring,
and delete the edges of $L(G)$ whose ends have the same colour.  What remains maps
to $K_2\to H$.  At a vertex with colour counts $a$ and $d-a$ the deleted edges
number $\binom a2+\binom{d-a}2=\binom d2-a(d-a)$.  This equals
$\binom d2-\lfloor d^2/4\rfloor$ when the colouring is balanced.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:balanced}
Balanced $2$-edge-colourings exist in three cases.  If $4\mid d$, split a
$2$-factorization (Petersen's $2$-factor theorem~\cite{petersen1891}) evenly.  If
$G$ is bipartite, group the $d$ colour classes of a proper edge colouring
(K\H{o}nig~\cite{konig1916}).  If $d$ is odd and $G$ has a perfect matching $M$,
split $G-M$ into $(d-1)/2$ two-factors.  Then give colour $1$ to $M$ together with
$\lfloor (d-1)/4\rfloor$ of these two-factors.

In particular every bridgeless cubic graph $G$ on $n$ vertices has a perfect
matching, by Petersen's theorem~\cite{petersen1891}.  So $|L(G),H|=n$ for every
triangle-free $H$ with an edge, and the whole ladder
$|L(G),C_5|=|L(G),C_7|=\dots=|L(G),K_2|$ collapses.

These two classical theorems are not formalized.  The Lean proof establishes the
cubic case for every cubic graph that has a perfect matching.
\end{remark}

For graphs with a proper $d$-edge-colouring the lower bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:line} is exact for every target, not only for $r=2$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:line_exact}
Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices with a proper $d$-edge-colouring, and let $H$
have clique number $r\ge1$.  Then
\[
  |L(G),H| = n\left(\binom d2-t(d,r)\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:line}.  For the upper bound, give each edge of
$G$ its colour modulo $r$ and map $L(G)$ to $K_r\subseteq H$, deleting the
monochromatic pairs.  At a vertex $v$ the $d$ incident edges carry the $d$
colours once each, so the induced colouring of the clique $Q_v$ is a relabelled
Tur\'an graph $T(d,r)$, and exactly $\binom d2-t(d,r)$ pairs at $v$ are deleted.
\end{proof}

By K\H{o}nig's theorem~\cite{konig1916} every bipartite regular graph has such a
colouring.  This gives exact ground-state energies of the antiferromagnetic
$q$-state Potts model, and of every constrained spin model with target $H$, on
the corner-sharing lattices.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:lattices}
Let $G$ be a finite bipartite $d$-regular graph on $n$ vertices, for example a
honeycomb, square or diamond lattice on a torus with even periods.  For every
$H$ with clique number $r$,
\[
  |L(G),H| = n\left(\binom d2-t(d,r)\right).
\]
For the kagome lattice $L(\text{honeycomb})$ this is $1$ unsatisfied bond per
triangle for $r=2$ and $0$ for $r\ge3$.  For the pyrochlore lattice
$L(\text{diamond})$ and the checkerboard lattice $L(\text{square})$ it is $2$ per
tetrahedron, respectively crossed square, for $r=2$, $1$ for $r=3$ and $0$ for
$r\ge4$.
\end{corollary}

The Ising values are the classical counts of one frustrated bond per kagome
triangle and of the ice rule on pyrochlore.  What is new is the exact value for
every target at once.  In particular the $3$-state Potts antiferromagnet on
pyrochlore has exactly one unsatisfied bond per tetrahedron.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:square_cycle}
Let $C_n^2$ ($n\ge5$) be the circulant graph on $\mathbb Z_n$ with jumps $1$ and
$2$.  For every triangle-free $H$, $|C_n^2,H|\ge\lceil n/2\rceil$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The $n$ triangles $\{i,i+1,i+2\}$ must each lose an edge.  An edge
$\{i,i+1\}$ lies only in the triangles starting at $i-1$ and at $i$, and an edge
$\{i,i+2\}$ lies only in the triangle starting at $i$.  So each deleted edge
destroys at most two of these triangles.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Triangular graphs}

The triangular graph $L(K_n)$ is $(2n-4)$-regular, and Theorem~\ref{thm:line} gives
$|L(K_n),K_2|\ge n\lfloor(n-2)^2/4\rfloor$.  Parity decides when this is exact.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:triangular}
For $n\ge2$,
\[
  |L(K_n),K_2| = n\left\lfloor\frac{(n-2)^2}{4}\right\rfloor + [\,n\equiv3\pmod 4\,].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A map $L(K_n)-S\to K_2$ is a $2$-colouring of $E(K_n)$.  If vertex $v$ has $a_v$
edges of colour $0$, then $S$ must contain the
$\binom{n-1}2-a_v(n-1-a_v)\ge\lfloor(n-2)^2/4\rfloor$ monochromatic pairs at $v$.
Conversely, deleting exactly the monochromatic pairs suffices.  Equality at $v$
needs $a_v$ to be balanced.  For $n\equiv3\pmod4$ this means $a_v=(n-1)/2$, which is
odd, at an odd number $n$ of vertices.  That contradicts the handshake lemma,
so some vertex costs at least one more.

For the upper bounds: if $n$ is even, colour $uw$ by the parity of $u+w$.  If
$n=2m+1$, give colour $0$ to the pairs at circular distance at most
$\lceil m/2\rceil$.  When $m$ is odd, remove the near-perfect matching
$\{0,1\},\{2,3\},\dots,\{2m-2,2m-1\}$ from colour~$0$.  Then every vertex is
balanced except the vertex $2m$.
\end{proof}

So $|L(K_n),K_2|=1,4,10,24,43,72,108$ for $n=3,\dots,9$.  For $n=7$ an
exhaustive search shows that no triangle-free target at all reaches the
line-graph bound $42$.  Every $K_{3,3}$-balanced choice of kept edges at the
seven vertices contains a triangle.  Hence $|L(K_7),H|=43$ for every triangle-free
$H$ with an edge; this search is not formalized.

A SAT computation extends this to $n=11$.  Call a vertex $u$ of a triple $T$ a
separator if the local bipartition at $u$ splits the other two vertices of $T$.
Kept edges reaching the bound form a triangle-free graph exactly when no triple has
three separators.  For balanced local bipartitions, a parity argument shows that
having no triple with one or three separators is impossible when
$n\equiv3\pmod4$; this is the handshake argument above.  The solver CaDiCaL
shows that, for odd $n\le13$, no triple with three separators forces no triple
with one separator.  For $n=11$ this takes $0.2$ seconds, and two other solvers
agree.  Hence $|L(K_{11}),H|=|L(K_{11}),K_2|=221$ for every triangle-free $H$
with an edge.  Simulated annealing never found fewer than one fully separated
triple for $n=15$, $19$ and $23$.

For $r\ge3$ the excess is a property of the target $K_r$, not of clique number
$r$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:triangular_general}
Let $n\ge1$ and $r\ge3$.  Then there is a graph $K$ of clique number $r$ with
\[
  |L(K_n),K| = n\left(\binom{n-1}2-t(n-1,r)\right).
\]
So the minimum of $|L(K_n),H|$ over all $H$ of clique number $r$ is the Turán
value, also in the exceptional case of Theorem~\ref{thm:triangular_r}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At every vertex $v$ of $K_n$ choose an equitable $r$-colouring $c_v$ of the other
$n-1$ vertices.  Let $K$ be the graph on $E(K_n)$ in which $vu\sim vw$ when
$c_v(u)\ne c_v(w)$.  The identity map sends $L(K_n)$ minus the monochromatic pairs
to $K$, and there are $n\bigl(\binom{n-1}2-t(n-1,r)\bigr)$ such pairs.  Four or
more edges of $K_n$ that pairwise meet form a star, so every clique of $K$ with
more than three vertices lies in one star, where $K$ is complete $r$-partite.
Since $r\ge3$, $K$ has clique number $r$.  The lower bound is
Theorem~\ref{thm:line}.
\end{proof}

The same parity phenomenon governs every number of colours.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:triangular_r}
Let $n\ge3$, $r\ge2$, and write $n-1=qr+s$ with $0\le s<r$.  Then
\[
  |L(K_n),K_r| = n\left(\binom{n-1}2-t(n-1,r)\right) + \frac r2\,
  \bigl[\,n \text{ odd},\ s=0,\ q \text{ odd}\,\bigr].
\]
Equivalently, the max-$r$-cut of the triangular graph $J(n,2)$ is
$n\,t(n-1,r)$, minus $r/2$ in the exceptional case.  For even $n$ the value
$n\bigl(\binom{n-1}2-t(n-1,r)\bigr)$ holds for every target of clique number $r$.
Outside the exceptional case the same is true for odd $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A map $L(K_n)-S\to K_r$ is an $r$-colouring of $E(K_n)$, and $S$ contains all
monochromatic pairs at each vertex: $\sum_c\binom{d_c(v)}2$ of them at $v$, where
$d_c(v)$ is the number of edges of colour $c$ at $v$.  This is at least
$\binom{n-1}2-t(n-1,r)$, with equality exactly when the $d_c(v)$ are
equitable.

In the exceptional case every $d_c(v)$ would have to equal $q$, but then colour
class $c$ would have odd degree sum $nq$.  Writing $x_c(v)=d_c(v)-q$, the excess at
$v$ is $\frac12\sum_cx_c(v)^2$, and every colour $c$ needs some $v$ with
$x_c(v)$ odd.  So the total excess is at least $r/2$.

For the constructions:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $n$ is even, colour a $1$-factorization of $K_n$ modulo $r$ and apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:line_exact}.
\item If $r\nmid n-1$, write $n-1=kr+s$ with $0<s<r$, put $B=\{0,\dots,kr-1\}$ and
$A=\{kr,\dots,n-1\}$, and let $\theta(x)=\lfloor x/2\rfloor+(x\bmod2)\lceil r/2\rceil$,
a permutation of $\mathbb Z_r$ with $2\theta(x)\in\{x,x-1\}$.  Colour $uw$ by
$\theta(u)+\theta(w)\bmod r$ inside $B$, by $(w-kr)+u-1\bmod r$ for $u\in B$,
$w\in A$, and by $(u-kr)+(w-kr)\bmod(s+1)$ inside $A$.  Every colour then occurs
$k$ or $k+1$ times at every vertex.  This explicit colouring replaces the
existence theorem of Hilton and de Werra~\cite{hiltondewerra1994}.
\item If $n=2m+1$ is odd and $s=0$, use the centre classes: the edges $uw$ with
$u+w\equiv2h\pmod n$ form a near-perfect matching missing $h$.  Give each colour
$q$ of the classes $h\ne0$, choosing the colours of $h$ and $-h$ equal for as
many $h$ as possible, and colour an edge $\{u,-u\}$ of class $0$ like the class
of its smaller endpoint.  A vertex then sees every colour $q$ times, except
that a pair $h,-h$ of differently coloured classes costs one unit.  If $q$ is
even no such pair is needed.  If $q$ is odd, exactly $r/2$ pairs are needed.
This replaces the Walecki decomposition~\cite{lucas1892}.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

For $r=2$ this is Theorem~\ref{thm:triangular}.  For $r=n-1$ and odd $n$ it gives
$|L(K_n),K_{n-1}|=(n-1)/2$, so $K_n$ falls short of an $(n-1)$-edge-colouring by
exactly $(n-1)/2$ monochromatic pairs.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:spectral}
The Mohar--Poljak eigenvalue bound $\frac14|V|\lambda_{\max}(L)$ on the max-cut
of $J(n,2)$ equals $\frac14n(n-1)^2$.  It is attained exactly when
$n\equiv1\pmod4$, it exceeds the max-cut by $1$ when $n\equiv3\pmod4$, and by
$n/4$ when $n$ is even.  For the Hamming graph $H(d,q)$ the eigenvalue bound
$\frac{k-1}{2k}|V|\lambda_{\max}(L)$ on the max-$k$-cut is attained exactly when
$k\mid q$, and otherwise exceeds it by $d\,q^{d-1}e(k-e)/(2k)$, where
$e=q\bmod k$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For a $k$-colouring $c$ with indicator vectors $x_1,\dots,x_k$, the cut size is
$\frac12\sum_ix_i^{\mathsf T}Lx_i$.  If $x^{\mathsf T}Lx\le\lambda\|x\|^2$ for all
$x$, applying this to $x_i-\frac1k\mathbf 1$ gives the bound
$\frac{k-1}{2k}\lambda|V|$.  A cover of the edges by cliques $C_j$, each vertex in
$\rho$ cliques of size $s$, gives $x^{\mathsf T}Lx=\rho s\|x\|^2-\sum_j(\sum_{C_j}x)^2
\le\rho s\|x\|^2$.  The stars of $K_n$ cover $J(n,2)$ with $\rho=2$, $s=n-1$; the
lines cover $H(d,q)$ with $\rho=d$, $s=q$.  So $\lambda=2n-2$ and $\lambda=dq$
are valid.  A vector supported on a $4$-cycle, or on two coordinates, shows that no
smaller $\lambda$ is valid.  Compare with Theorems~\ref{thm:triangular}
and~\ref{thm:hamming}, using $t(q,k)\le\frac{k-1}{2k}q^2$ with equality iff
$k\mid q$.
\end{proof}

Both graphs are walk-regular, so for $k=2$ the eigenvalue bound coincides with the
Goemans--Williamson semidefinite bound~\cite{vandam2016}.  So $J(n,2)$ with
$n\equiv3\pmod4$ is an explicit family whose semidefinite integrality gap is
exactly one edge.  The proof above uses no eigenvalue theory, and it is formalized
as stated.

\subsection{Tensor products}

The tensor product $G\times H$ has vertex set $V(G)\times V(H)$, with
$(g,h)\sim(g',h')$ if and only if $g\sim g'$ and $h\sim h'$.

\begin{theorem}[Quantitative Hedetniemi bound]
\label{thm:hedetniemi}
For all graphs $G$, $H$ and every graph $K$ with at least one vertex,
\[
  |G\times H,K|\;\le\;\min\bigl(2|E(H)|\cdot|G,K|,\;2|E(G)|\cdot|H,K|\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The projection $G\times H\to G$ sends exactly $2|E(H)|$ edges onto each edge of
$G$, one for each orientation of each edge of $H$.  Now apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:nhf_bound} to this map.  If $G\times H$ has no edges it maps to
any graph with a vertex.  The second bound is symmetric.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis on $K$ is needed.  If $K$ has no vertices and $H$ has no edges,
the right-hand side is $0$ but the left-hand side is infinite.  By
Shitov's counterexample to Hedetniemi's conjecture~\cite{shitov2019}, the bound
can be far from tight for large targets: the left-hand side can be $0$ while
neither factor is $k$-colourable.  For two odd cycles it is tight, and not only
for $K=K_2$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:CpCq}
Let $3\le p\le q$ be odd and let $H$ have an edge.  Then
\[
  |C_p\times C_q,H|=\begin{cases}2p & \text{if } C_q\not\to H,\\
  0 & \text{if } C_q\to H.\end{cases}
\]
In particular $|C_p\times C_q,C_m|=2p$ for every odd $m>q$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $C_q\to H$, compose with the projection $C_p\times C_q\to C_q$.  Otherwise
$|C_p\times C_q,H|\le|C_p\times C_q,K_2|$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:monotone}, since
$K_2\to H$.  The right-hand side is at most $2p$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:hedetniemi},
since $|C_q,K_2|=1$.

For the lower bound, let $\varepsilon_i=+1$ for $0\le i<(q+p)/2$ and
$\varepsilon_i=-1$ for $(q+p)/2\le i<q$, and let $g_i=\sum_{j<i}\varepsilon_j$.
Then $g_q=p\equiv0\pmod p$.  For $a\in\mathbb Z_p$, $h\in\mathbb Z_q$ and
$\sigma\in\{\pm1\}$, the walk $i\mapsto(a+\sigma g_i,\,h+i)$, $0\le i\le q$, is a
closed walk of length $q$ in $C_p\times C_q$, that is, a homomorphism
$C_q\to C_p\times C_q$.  If one of these $2pq$ walks avoided the deletion set $S$,
composing with $C_p\times C_q-S\to H$ would give $C_q\to H$.  So each walk
contains a deleted edge.

Fix a step index $i$ and an edge $e$.  The second coordinate increases by $1$
along every step, so it determines which end of $e$ the step leaves from.  The
first coordinate then determines $\sigma\varepsilon_i$, hence $\sigma$, and then
$a$ and $h$.  So at most one walk uses $e$ at step $i$.  Charging each walk to
the step at which it meets the deletion set $S$ gives $2pq\le q|S|$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Petersen colourings}

A \emph{Petersen colouring} of a cubic graph $G$ is a map $E(G)\to E(P)$ that
sends the three edges at each vertex onto the three edges at some vertex of $P$.
Since $P$ is triangle-free, every triangle of $L(P)$ is the star of a vertex.  So
Petersen colourings of $G$ are exactly the homomorphisms $L(G)\to L(P)$, and
Jaeger's Petersen colouring conjecture~\cite{jaeger1988} says that
$|L(G),L(P)|=0$ for every bridgeless cubic $G$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:jaeger}
Let $G$ be cubic.  Let $X_1,\dots,X_t\subseteq V(G)$ be pairwise disjoint sets,
each of which is left by exactly one edge of $G$.  Then $|L(G),L(P)|\ge t$.  In
particular $|L(G),L(P)|$ is at least the number of leaves of the bridge-block tree
of $G$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\varphi\colon L(G)-S\to L(P)$.  Call $x\in V(G)$ \emph{good} if none of
the three edges of $L(G)$ between edges at $x$ lies in $S$.  For a good $x$, the
images of the three edges at $x$ form a triangle of $L(P)$, which is a star.

$P$ has exactly six perfect matchings, and each edge lies in exactly two of them.
Fix $X=X_i$, its leaving edge $b$, and a perfect matching $M$.  Each good $x$ has
exactly one incident edge whose image is in $M$.  If every vertex of $X$ is good,
counting incidences gives $|X|=2m+[\varphi(b)\in M]$, where $m$ is the number of
edges inside $X$ with image in $M$.  But $3|X|=2|E(X)|+1$, so $|X|$ is odd and
$\varphi(b)\in M$.  This holds for all six matchings, which is impossible.

So each $X_i$ contains a bad vertex $x_i$, together with an edge
$\{e_i,f_i\}\in S$ of $L(G)$ where $e_i,f_i$ are distinct edges at $x_i$.  Two
distinct edges of $G$ share at most one vertex, so these $t$ elements of $S$ are
distinct.  The vertex sets of the leaf blocks of the bridge-block tree satisfy
the hypothesis, each being left only by its bridge.
\end{proof}

\section{The Concentration Parameter}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{definition}[Concentration parameter]
\label{def:gamma}
For a homomorphism $f\colon G\to H$ and $e\in E(H)$, the \emph{load} $\mu(f,e)$
is the number of edges of $G$ mapped onto $e$.  Let
$\mu(f)=\max_{e}\mu(f,e)$ and
\[
  \gamma(G,H) = \min_{f\colon G\to H} \mu(f),
\]
with $\gamma(G,H)=+\infty$ when $G\not\to H$.
\end{definition}

A related per-homomorphism quantity $M^\sigma$ appears in~\cite{daneshgar2007}.

\begin{proposition}[Pigeonhole bound]
\label{prop:pigeonhole}
If $G\to H$ then $|E(G)|\le|E(H)|\cdot\gamma(G,H)$, so
$\gamma(G,H)\ge\lceil|E(G)|/|E(H)|\rceil$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The loads of $f$ sum to $|E(G)|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:gamma_basic}
If $|E(G)|\ge1$ and $G\to H$ then $\gamma(G,H)\ge1$.  Moreover $\gamma(G,G)=1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Multiplicativity]
\label{lem:gamma_mult}
If $G\to H\to K$ then $\gamma(G,K)\le\gamma(G,H)\cdot\gamma(H,K)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Take optimal $f\colon G\to H$ and $g\colon H\to K$.  An edge $e$ of $K$ has at
most $\mu(g)$ preimage edges in $H$, and each of these has at most $\mu(f)$
preimages in $G$.  So $\mu(g\circ f)\le\mu(f)\mu(g)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Transfer bound]
\label{lem:nhf_bound}
If $G\to H$ and $|E(G)|\ge1$, then $|G,K|\le\gamma(G,H)\cdot|H,K|$ for every $K$.
More generally $|G,K|\le\mu(f)\,|H,K|$ for every homomorphism $f\colon G\to H$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $H-S\to K$ with $|S|=|H,K|$, and let $T$ be the set of edges of $G$ that $f$
maps into $S$.  Then $|T|\le\mu(f)|S|$, and $f$ restricts to
$G-T\to H-S\to K$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Two-sided bound]
\label{cor:two_sided}
If $G\to H$ and $K\to H$, and both have an edge, then
$\max(|G,K|,|K,G|)\le\max\bigl(\gamma(G,H)|H,K|,\ \gamma(K,H)|H,G|\bigr)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{lemma}[Monotonicity in the target]
\label{lem:gamma_mono}
If $H_1$ embeds injectively in $H_2$ (for instance $H_1\subseteq H_2$), then
$\gamma(G,H_2)\le\gamma(G,H_1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Composing with an injective homomorphism does not change any load.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:gamma_Kn}
For $2\le n\le m$, $\gamma(K_n,K_m)=1$.  For $n>m$, $\gamma(K_n,K_m)=+\infty$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{An exact identity for homomorphisms to $K_3$}

For $f\colon G\to K_3$ with colour classes $A_0,A_1,A_2$, let
$D_i=\sum_{v\in A_i}\deg v$ and write $L_{ij}=\mu(f,\{i,j\})$.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:degree_identity}
For every graph $G$, every $f\colon G\to K_3$ and distinct colours $a,b,c$,
\[
  2L_{ab}=D_a+D_b-D_c,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  2\mu(f)=\max(D_0+D_1-D_2,\;D_0+D_2-D_1,\;D_1+D_2-D_0).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each edge at a vertex of $A_a$ goes to $A_b$ or to $A_c$, so $D_a=L_{ab}+L_{ac}$.
Solve the three equations.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[$d$-regularity formula]
\label{thm:d_regular}
Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices with $d\ge1$, and let $f\colon G\to K_3$
have colour classes of sizes $a,b,c$.  Then
\[
  2\mu(f) = d\cdot\max(a+b-c,\; a+c-b,\; b+c-a).
\]
The triple $(a+b-c,a+c-b,b+c-a)$ is non-negative, sums to $n$, and has constant
parity.  Hence, if $M$ is the least maximum of such a triple, then
$\gamma(G,K_3)\ge\frac d2M$.  Equality holds as soon as some proper $3$-colouring
attains $M$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $D_i=d|A_i|$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:degree_identity}.  Non-negativity follows from
$d\,(a+b-c)=2L_{ab}\ge0$, and the entries differ by even numbers.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:cycle_gamma}
For all $n\ge3$,
\[
  \gamma(C_n, K_3) =
  \begin{cases}
    \lceil n/3 \rceil     & \text{if } n \not\equiv 2 \pmod{3}, \\
    \lceil n/3 \rceil + 1 & \text{if } n \equiv 2 \pmod{3}.
  \end{cases}
\]
In particular $\gamma(C_n,K_3)$ attains the pigeonhole bound if and only if
$n\not\equiv2\pmod 3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular} with $d=2$ and write $n=3q+r$.  A same-parity
triple summing to $n$ has maximum at least $\lceil n/3\rceil$.  For $r=2$ the
only triple with maximum $q+1$ is $(q+1,q+1,q)$, which has mixed parity, so the
maximum is at least $q+2$.

To see that these bounds are attained, colour vertex $i<3q$ by $i\bmod3$.  If
$r=1$, give the last vertex colour~$1$.  If $r=2$, give the last two vertices
colours $0$ and $1$.  The class sizes are $(q,q,q)$, $(q,q+1,q)$ and
$(q+1,q+1,q)$, with maxima $q$, $q+1$ and $q+2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:Knn}
$|K_{n,n},K_3|=0$ and $\gamma(K_{n,n},K_3)=n\lceil n/2\rceil$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $f\colon K_{n,n}\to K_3$.  The colour sets of the two sides are disjoint, so
one side is monochromatic in a colour that the other side does not use.  That
colour class has exactly $n$ vertices.  With $d=n$ and class sizes
$(x,n-x,n)$, Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular} gives $\mu(f)=n\max(x,n-x)\ge
n\lceil n/2\rceil$.  Splitting one side as evenly as possible attains this.
\end{proof}

For $n=1,2,3,4$ this gives $1,2,6,8$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:petersen_gamma}
$\gamma(P,K_3)=6$ and $\gamma(P,K_5)=2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $K_3$ take $d=3$ and $n=10$.  Same-parity triples summing to $10$ are all
even, so their maximum is at least $4$, and $\gamma(P,K_3)\ge6$.  Equality holds
because $P$ has a proper $3$-colouring with class sizes $(3,4,3)$, whose triple is
$(4,2,4)$.  For $K_5$, the pigeonhole bound $\lceil15/10\rceil=2$ is attained by
an explicit $5$-colouring.
\end{proof}

So $\gamma(P,K_3)=6$ exceeds the pigeonhole bound $5$ because of parity.  In
contrast $\gamma(P,K_5)$ attains it.

\subsection{Equitable colourings and regular graphs}

A proper colouring is \emph{equitable} if its class sizes differ pairwise by at
most one.  Write $M(n)=\lceil n/3\rceil+[n\equiv2\pmod 3]$.  This is the least
maximum of a same-parity triple of non-negative integers summing to $n$, and it is
the value found in Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle_gamma}.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:equitable}
Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices, $d\ge1$, with an equitable proper
$3$-colouring.  Then
\[
  \gamma(G,K_3)=\frac d2\,M(n),
\]
and every equitable $3$-colouring is optimal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular} with $M=M(n)$.  If the class sizes
differ by at most one, the triple $(a+b-c,a+c-b,b+c-a)$ is $(q,q,q)$,
$(q+1,q+1,q-1)$ or $(q+2,q,q)$ for $n=3q$, $3q+1$, $3q+2$.  Its maximum is
$M(n)$, so the identity of Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular} gives
$2\mu(f)=d\,M(n)$.
\end{proof}

Chen, Lih and Wu~\cite{chenlihwu1994} proved, as a case of their equitable
$\Delta$-colouring theorem, that every connected cubic graph other than $K_4$ and
$K_{3,3}$ has an equitable $3$-colouring.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:cubic}
Every connected cubic graph $G$ other than $K_4$ and $K_{3,3}$ satisfies
\[
  \gamma(G,K_3)=3\left\lceil\frac{|V(G)|}{6}\right\rceil .
\]
Moreover $\gamma(K_{3,3},K_3)=6$ and $\gamma(K_4,K_3)=\infty$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $n=|V(G)|$ even, $\frac32M(n)=3\lceil n/6\rceil$.  Apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:equitable}.  The value for $K_{3,3}$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:Knn}.
\end{proof}

In particular every prism $C_k\square K_2$ ($k\ge3$), every M\"obius ladder on
$2k\ge8$ vertices, every generalized Petersen graph and the Petersen graph have
$\gamma(G,K_3)=3\lceil|V(G)|/6\rceil$.  By contrast, the pigeonhole bound is only
$|V(G)|/2$.  A computer check of all $45\,982$ connected cubic graphs on at most
$18$ vertices confirms that $K_4$ and $K_{3,3}$ are the only exceptions.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:cube}
For $d\ge1$,
\[
  \gamma(Q_d,K_3)=\begin{cases} d\,(2^d+2)/6 & d \text{ even},\\
  d\,(2^d+4)/6 & d\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Colour $x\in\{0,1\}^d$ by its weight mod $3$.  Adjacent words differ in weight by
one, so this is proper.  Let $N_c(d)$ count the words of weight $\equiv c$.  These
counts satisfy $N_c(d+1)=N_c(d)+N_{c-1}(d)$, so
$N_c(d+1)-N_{c+1}(d+1)=N_{c-1}(d)-N_{c+1}(d)$, and by induction the three counts
differ pairwise by at most one.  Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:equitable} with
$n=2^d$, using $2^d\equiv1$ or $2\pmod 3$ according as $d$ is even or odd.
\end{proof}

This gives $2,6,12,30$ for $d=2,3,4,5$.  Tori behave the same way: for
example, colouring $(i,j)\mapsto i+j\bmod 3$ shows that
$\gamma(C_{3a}\square C_{3b},K_3)=6ab$ attains the pigeonhole bound.

\subsection{Joins with an independent set}

The \emph{join} $G_1+\overline{K_k}$ is obtained from $G_1$ by adding $k$
pairwise non-adjacent vertices, each joined to every vertex of $G_1$.  For a
bipartite $G_1$, let $\beta(G_1)$ be the least possible size of the larger class
of a proper $2$-colouring of $G_1$.  Put $\beta(G_1)=\infty$ if $G_1$ is not
bipartite.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:join}
Let $G_1$ have at least one edge and let $k\ge1$.  Then
\[
  \gamma(G_1+\overline{K_k},K_3)=\max\bigl(|E(G_1)|,\;k\,\beta(G_1)\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $f\colon G_1+\overline{K_k}\to K_3$, and let $c$ be the colour of one new
vertex.  No vertex of $G_1$ has colour $c$.  If another new vertex had a colour
$c'\ne c$, the two ends of an edge of $G_1$ would need two distinct colours
outside $\{c,c'\}$, which is impossible.  So all new vertices have colour $c$, and
$f$ restricts to a $2$-colouring of $G_1$ with classes $A$ and $B$ in the other
colours $a,b$.  Every edge of $G_1$ maps onto $ab$, and the edges at the new
vertices give loads $k|A|$ on $ac$ and $k|B|$ on $bc$.  Hence
$\mu(f)=\max(|E(G_1)|,k|A|,k|B|)$.  Minimising over $2$-colourings of $G_1$ gives
the formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:join}
$\gamma(B_k,K_3)=k$, $\gamma(F_k,K_3)=k$ and $\gamma(W_n,K_3)=n-1$ for odd
$n\ge5$.  Also $\gamma(K_{p,q,r},K_3)=\max(pq,r\max(p,q))$ for $p,q,r\ge1$, which
equals $\max(pq,pr,qr)$.  For the fan $P_t+K_1$ with $t\ge3$,
$\gamma=t-1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Take $G_1=K_2$ and $k$ new vertices for $B_k$.  Take $G_1=kK_2$ or $C_{n-1}$ with
one new vertex for $F_k$ and $W_n$.  Take $G_1=K_{p,q}$ for $K_{p,q,r}$, and
$G_1=P_t$ for the fan.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Irregular source graphs}

Theorem~\ref{thm:families} gives a direct proof of the first four cases of
Corollary~\ref{cor:join}, with the values of $|\cdot,K_2|$ as well.

The \emph{book} $B_k$ is $k$ triangles sharing one edge (the spine).  The
\emph{friendship graph} $F_k$ is $k$ triangles sharing one vertex.  The
\emph{wheel} $W_n$ is a vertex (the hub) joined to every vertex of $C_{n-1}$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:families}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For $k\ge1$: $\gamma(B_k,K_3)=k$ and $|B_k,K_2|=1$.
  \item For $k\ge0$: $\gamma(F_k,K_3)=k$ and $|F_k,K_2|=k$.
  \item For odd $n\ge5$: $\gamma(W_n,K_3)=n-1$.
  \item For $p,q,r\ge1$: $\gamma(K_{p,q,r},K_3)=\max(pq,pr,qr)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All four use Lemma~\ref{lem:degree_identity}.

(1) The two spine vertices get distinct colours $a,b$, and every page vertex gets
the third colour $c$.  So $D_a=D_b=k+1$ and $D_c=2k$, giving $L_{ab}=1$ and
$L_{ac}=L_{bc}=k$.  Deleting the spine leaves a bipartite graph, and $B_k$
contains a triangle, so $|B_k,K_2|=1$.

(2) The hub gets some colour $c$.  Each triangle puts one vertex in each of the
other two classes, so $D_0=D_1=D_2=2k$ and every load equals $k$, for every
homomorphism.  The $k$ triangles are edge-disjoint, so $|F_k,K_2|\ge k$.
Deleting the $k$ edges opposite the hub leaves a star.

(3) The hub, of colour $c$, is alone in its class, so $D_c=n-1$.  The total degree
is $4(n-1)$, so $D_a+D_b=3(n-1)$ and $L_{ab}=n-1$.  Alternating two colours on
the even rim attains this.

(4) If all parts are non-empty, each part is monochromatic and the three parts
get distinct colours.  Otherwise some vertex would see three colours among its
neighbours.  The class of part $i$ has $D=s_i(N-s_i)$, where $N=p+q+r$, so
$2L=s_i(N-s_i)+s_j(N-s_j)-s_k(N-s_k)=2s_is_j$.
\end{proof}

In particular $\gamma(F_k,K_3)$ attains the pigeonhole bound $k$.  On the other
hand $\gamma(W_n,K_3)=n-1$ exceeds it for $n\ge5$, and so do
$\gamma(K_{1,2,3},K_3)=6>4$ and $\gamma(K_{2,3,4},K_3)=12>9$.  The book graphs
separate the two parameters: $|B_k,K_2|=1$ for every $k$, while
$\gamma(B_k,K_3)=k$ grows without bound.

\subsection{Cycles into odd cycles}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:cycle_cycle}
Let $m\ge3$ be odd and $n\ge3$, and suppose $C_n\to C_m$.  Then $\gamma(C_n,C_m)$
is the least integer $M$ such that $M\equiv n\pmod 2$ and $mM\ge n$.  Thus it is
$\lceil n/m\rceil$ or $\lceil n/m\rceil+1$, whichever has the parity of $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix an edge $e=\{x,x+1\}$ of $C_m$.  Number the vertices of the path $C_m-e$ from
$x+1$ to $x$ as $0,1,\dots,m-1$.  Along the closed walk $f(0),f(1),\dots$ the
position changes parity at every step except a step across $e$, where it goes
between $0$ and the even number $m-1$.  A closed walk changes parity an even
number of times, so $n-\mu(f,e)$ is even.  Hence every load, and in particular
$\mu(f)$, has the parity of $n$.  The pigeonhole bound gives $m\mu(f)\ge n$.

For the upper bound write $n=wm+2s$ with $w\in\{0,1\}$ the parity of $n$.  Walk
once around $C_m$ if $w=1$.  Then walk $s$ steps forward and $s$ steps back.  Each
edge is used at most $w+2\lceil s/m\rceil\le M$ times.
\end{proof}

For $m=3$, where $C_3=K_3$, this is Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle_gamma}.  The smallest
$M$ of the parity of $n$ with $3M\ge n$ is $\lceil n/3\rceil$, except that it is
$\lceil n/3\rceil+1$ when $n\equiv2\pmod 3$.  For $m=5$ and $m=7$ it gives, for
example, $\gamma(C_6,C_5)=2$, $\gamma(C_{12},C_5)=4$, $\gamma(C_9,C_7)=3$ and
$\gamma(C_{15},C_7)=3$.  So the pigeonhole bound $\lceil n/m\rceil$ is attained
exactly when $\lceil n/m\rceil\equiv n\pmod 2$.

The parity phenomenon extends from cycles to all regular graphs.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:regular_oddcycle}
Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices, $d\ge1$, and let $m\ge3$ be odd.  For
$f\colon G\to C_m$ put $n_c=|f^{-1}(c)|$ and
$s_c=\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}(-1)^t\,n_{c-t}$.  Then the load of the edge $\{c,c+1\}$ is
$\frac d2\,s_c$.  Consequently
\[
  \gamma(G,C_m)\;\ge\;\frac d2\,M_m(n),
\]
where $M_m(n)$ is the least $M\equiv n\pmod 2$ with $mM\ge n$.  Equality holds
whenever some $f$ has $s_c\le M_m(n)$ for all $c$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $L_c$ for the load of $\{c,c+1\}$.  Every edge at a vertex of colour $c$
goes to colour $c\pm1$, so $d\,n_c=L_{c-1}+L_c$.  Telescoping this relation
once around the odd cycle gives $2L_c=\sum_t(-1)^t d\,n_{c-t}=d\,s_c$.  Hence
$s_c\ge0$, $s_c+s_{c-1}=2n_c$, so all $s_c$ have the same parity, and
$\sum_cs_c=\frac2d|E(G)|=n$.  The largest $s_c$ therefore satisfies
$s_c\equiv n\pmod 2$ and $m\,s_c\ge n$, so it is at least $M_m(n)$.
\end{proof}

For $m=3$ this is Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular}, since $s_c$ is then
$a+b-c$ in the notation used there.  For $d=2$ it is the lower bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle_cycle}.  A computer search finds the bound exact for the
tori $C_5\square C_5$, $C_5\square C_7$ and $C_7\square C_7$ into $C_5$ and
$C_7\square C_7$ into $C_7$.  It is also exact for $412$ of the $465$ triangle-free
connected cubic graphs on $10$ to $16$ vertices that map to $C_5$; the other $53$ exceed it
by exactly $3$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:cycle_hom}
Let $m\ge3$ be odd and $n\ge3$.  Then $C_n\to C_m$ if and only if $n$ is even or
$n\ge m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An even cycle maps onto an edge.  If $n\ge m$ are both odd, wind once around
$C_m$ and then oscillate on an edge for the remaining even number of steps.
Conversely, let $n<m$ with $n$ odd and $f\colon C_n\to C_m$.  Then $f$ misses some
vertex $w$, and $C_m-w$ is a path.  So $C_n$ would be bipartite, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\section{The Symmetric Combination $m(G,H)$}
\label{sec:metric}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Let $m(G,H)=\max(|G,H|,|H,G|)$.  It is non-negative and symmetric, and
$m(G,G)=0$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:m_zero}
$m(G,H)=0$ if and only if $G\leftrightarrow H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:no_triangle}
$m$ does not satisfy the triangle inequality, and so is not a pseudo-metric.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:KnKm}, and since $K_n\to K_m$ for $n\le m$,
\[
  m(K_7,K_3)=5,\qquad m(K_7,K_5)=2,\qquad m(K_5,K_3)=2,
\]
and $5>2+2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Khoshkha~\cite{khoshkha2005} claims that $m$ is a pseudo-metric.
Theorem~\ref{thm:no_triangle} shows that this is false under
Definition~\ref{def:nhf}.
\end{remark}

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The exact values in this paper rarely beat the obvious designs by much.  Their
main use is as \emph{certified ground truth}: optima at any size, proved in Lean
for families of graphs and targets that people actually use.

\paragraph{Benchmarks for exact and heuristic solvers.}
Max-$k$-Cut, minimum-violation $H$-colouring, QUBO and Ising solvers, including
quantum annealers, are usually evaluated against best-known values or planted
solutions.  The families above give instances with proved optima, and one
instance serves every target with the same clique number.  The informative ones
are those where any relaxation or averaging argument stops short of the optimum,
so that a solver has to find a global proof:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Instance & optimum & naive bound \\
\midrule
$|L(K_7),K_2|$ & $43$ & $42$ \\
$\gamma(Q_{10},K_3)$ & $1710$ & $1707$ \\
$\gamma(Q_{11},K_3)$ & $3762$ & $3755$ \\
$\gamma(C_7\square C_7,C_5)$ & $22$ & $20$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Corollary~\ref{cor:cubic} adds an unlimited supply of irregular instances:
every random connected cubic graph other than $K_4$ and $K_{3,3}$ has
$\gamma(G,K_3)=3\lceil|V(G)|/6\rceil$.  This rests on the cited Chen--Lih--Wu
theorem.

\paragraph{Frustrated magnets.}
Corollary~\ref{cor:lattices} gives exact ground-state energies on kagome,
checkerboard and pyrochlore tori for every target.  The products
$C_p\times C_q$ with $p\le q$ odd carry a forced seam of exactly $2p$ unsatisfied
bonds for every target into which $C_q$ does not map (Theorem~\ref{thm:CpCq}).
These are natural test lattices for annealing and Monte Carlo methods.  The
theorems say nothing about ground-state degeneracy or entropy.

\paragraph{Placement across failure domains.}
Spreading the switches of a network over $m$ failure domains, so that as few
links as possible stay inside a domain, is exactly the problem $|G,K_m|$.  On
HyperX and flattened-butterfly fabrics, which are Hamming graphs,
Theorem~\ref{thm:hamming} gives the optimum.  For example $H(3,8)$ split into four
domains keeps exactly $768$ of its $5376$ links inside domains.  For folded cubes
the optimum $2^{n-2}$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:folded} is attained by removing the
antipodal links.  In a $d$-regular network whose links must all cross domains,
Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular} shows that the load between two domains depends only on
the domain sizes.

These applications do not cover locality-preserving partitioning, which minimises
the links between domains rather than inside them, or access patterns such as
strides and diagonals in memory-bank layouts.

\section{Open Questions}
\label{sec:open}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{General targets.}  Lemma~\ref{lem:degree_identity} determines
$\mu(f)$ for $f\colon G\to K_3$ from the degree sums of the colour classes.  Is
there an analogous identity for targets $H$ other than $K_3$?

\item \textbf{When is the pigeonhole bound attained?}  For cycles into $K_3$ it
is attained exactly when $n\not\equiv2\pmod3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle_gamma}).
For $d$-regular $G$, Theorem~\ref{thm:d_regular} turns the question into one about
realisable colour-class sizes.  Characterise the pairs $(G,H)$ for which
$\gamma(G,H)=\lceil|E(G)|/|E(H)|\rceil$.

\item \textbf{Tightness of the transitive bound.}  Theorem~\ref{thm:edge_transitive}
is tight for the Petersen graph and for $C_p\times C_q$.  For which
edge-transitive $G$ is $|G,K_2|$ equal to $|E(G)|$ divided by the odd girth?

\item \textbf{Joins with other targets.}  Is there a formula analogous to
Theorem~\ref{thm:join} for $\gamma(G_1+\overline{K_k},K_m)$ with $m\ge4$, or for
general joins $G_1+G_2$?

\item \textbf{Triangular graphs.}  Is $|L(K_n),H|=|L(K_n),K_2|$ for every
triangle-free $H$ with an edge?  By Theorem~\ref{thm:line} this holds for
$n\not\equiv3\pmod4$, and the computations after Theorem~\ref{thm:triangular}
confirm it for $n=7$ and $n=11$.  It would follow from this statement: for balanced
local bipartitions of $K_n$, if no triple has three separators then no triple has
one.  For $r\ge3$ the answer is negative by Theorem~\ref{thm:triangular_general}.

\item \textbf{Repairing $m$.}  Does the edge-edit distance to a graph mapping to
$H$, or $m$ restricted to graphs with the same number of vertices, satisfy the
triangle inequality?

\item \textbf{Petersen colourings.}  Is $|L(G),L(P)|$ equal to the number of leaf
blocks of the bridge-block tree for every cubic $G$?  The case of bridgeless $G$
is Jaeger's conjecture.

\item \textbf{Tightness of Theorem~\ref{thm:hedetniemi}.}  For which $G$, $H$ is
the bound tight for $K=K_2$ or $K=K_3$?  Theorem~\ref{thm:CpCq} gives an
infinite tight family for $K_2$.  For large targets the bound fails to be tight,
by~\cite{shitov2019}.
\end{enumerate}
